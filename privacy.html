Juncture Privacy Policy

Last updated: 26 October 2026

Juncture suggests what workout to do and when. To do that it needs to know when you are free and how recovered you are. This page explains exactly what it reads, where that information goes, and what it never does.

The short version: Juncture has no accounts, no servers of its own, and no analytics. Your health data never leaves your phone. Your location is sent to Apple — and only Apple — when Juncture looks up gyms near you. Nothing is ever sold, and nothing is ever used for advertising.

Juncture is published by Patrick Foley, an individual developer in the United States. You can reach us at patfoley4291@gmail.com.

There is no Juncture server

This is the most important thing to understand, because almost everything else follows from it.

the developer does not operate a backend. There is no database with your name in it, no login, no user ID, no profile. Juncture is a program that runs on your iPhone and reads data that is already on your iPhone. We cannot see what Juncture sees, because there is nowhere for it to send that information to us — and no code in the app that would do so.

That means some of the things a privacy policy normally promises — "you can ask us for a copy of your data", "we will delete your account" — do not apply in the usual way. There is no copy for us to send you and no account for us to delete. The section on your rights explains what this means in practice.

It also means this policy describes the app as it ships today. If a backend is ever added, this policy will change before that version is released.

Health data

What Juncture reads from Apple Health, with your permission, and only after you grant it:

  • Workouts (type, start time, duration, and active energy burned)
  • Sleep analysis
  • Heart-rate variability (SDNN)
  • Resting heart rate
  • Active energy

Juncture also calculates your own 30-day averages for heart-rate variability and resting heart rate, and keeps those as a baseline. It does this on your phone. A heart-rate variability of 40 ms is excellent for one person and a warning sign for another, so the only reading that means anything is the comparison to your own normal.

What Juncture does with it: scores how recovered you are, and uses that to pick the intensity and type of session to suggest. The reasoning it shows you on screen is generated from these numbers.

Where it goes: nowhere. This data is read into memory on your device, used to produce a recommendation, and that is the end of it. There is no code path in Juncture that transmits health data anywhere — not to us, not to Apple beyond the Health app it came from, not to anyone.

What Juncture will never do with health data, in line with Apple's rules for apps that use HealthKit, and as a commitment to you regardless of those rules:

  • Never use it for advertising or marketing of any kind.
  • Never sell it, and never share it with a data broker.
  • Never disclose it to a third party.

Juncture cannot write to Apple Health. This is not a promise about our intentions — the app's HealthKit entitlement requests read access only, and grants no write access at all. Nothing Juncture does can alter or add to your Health records.

If you decline Health access, or grant it and then revoke it, Juncture still works. It falls back to example content and tells you on screen that it is doing so. You can review or change what Juncture can read at any time in the Health app, under Sharing → Apps.

Calendar

Juncture needs to know when you are free, which means reading your calendar.

Your device's calendars

With your permission, Juncture reads the events in the calendars on your iPhone for today and the next seven days — including calendars you have subscribed through accounts like Google or Microsoft, because iOS presents those alongside the rest. It reads each event's title, start and end time, location if there is one, and whether you have declined the invitation (a meeting you turned down does not block your afternoon). It skips all-day events, which say nothing about whether you are busy.

It uses this to find the gaps long enough to train in. Event titles and locations appear on Juncture's Plan screen so you can see your day in context; they are not analysed for anything else.

Juncture also writes to your calendar, but only when you ask it to. Tapping "Hold it" creates a single event titled "Workout (held by Juncture)" in your default calendar, covering the window you held. Tapping "Add week to calendar" creates one event per planned session, titled after the session and ending in the same "(held by Juncture)". It writes nothing else, and it never modifies or deletes your existing events.

All of this happens on your device, through iOS's own calendar system. None of it is transmitted anywhere.

You can change or withdraw calendar access at any time in iOS Settings → Privacy & Security → Calendars.

Google Calendar (optional, and off unless you turn it on)

Juncture can connect directly to Google Calendar instead. When you connect a calendar, the app asks which one you want; nothing happens with Google unless you choose it and sign in.

If you do connect it:

  • Juncture signs you in using OAuth 2.0 with PKCE. You enter your Google password on Google's own sign-in page, inside Apple's secure web authentication view. Juncture never sees your Google password.
  • Juncture requests permission to read your calendars and to create events. It reads your primary Google calendar for the next seven days, and creates the same "(held by Juncture)" events when you hold a window or add the week.
  • These requests go over the internet to Google's servers (googleapis.com and oauth2.googleapis.com), carrying your access token and the date range being queried. This is the one case where calendar information leaves your device. It goes to Google, which already holds this calendar because it is your Google account — it does not come to us, and we have no way to see it. Google's handling of it is governed by Google's own privacy policy.
  • The long-lived refresh token that keeps you signed in is stored in the iOS Keychain, marked so that it stays on this one device: it is excluded from iCloud backups and does not sync to your other devices.

You can disconnect Google Calendar inside Juncture, which deletes that stored token from your device. Disconnecting in the app does not, by itself, withdraw the permission you gave at Google. To revoke that, go to myaccount.google.com → Security → Your connections to third-party apps, and remove Juncture. We recommend doing both.

Location

Juncture uses your location, with your permission, to show which gyms and studios are near you and how far away each one is. It asks for "when in use" access only — Juncture cannot see where you are when it is not open.

Your location is sent to Apple. We want to be precise about this, because it is the one piece of information Juncture handles that genuinely leaves your phone without you doing anything beyond opening the Map screen.

Juncture finds nearby gyms using MapKit, Apple's maps service. To return results, MapKit sends your coordinate to Apple's servers along with the search. Drawing the map itself also involves requesting map imagery for the area you are looking at. This is Apple's service, processing that request; Apple's handling of it is governed by Apple's privacy policy.

We never receive your location. It goes from your phone to Apple and the results come back. There is no step in between where we see it, store it, or could retrieve it.

A few details worth knowing:

  • Juncture asks for accuracy of roughly 100 metres, which is all that is needed to rank gyms by distance. It does not need or use your exact position.
  • If you decline location access, the Map screen shows example venues and says so. You can still search any city by name; that search sends the name you typed to Apple, not your location.
  • Opening a venue's "Apple Maps details" asks Apple for that place's card — hours, photos and reviews — using Apple's identifier for the venue. That request goes to Apple, not to us.
  • Tapping "Directions" opens Apple Maps with the gym as the destination. From that point you are in Apple Maps, and Apple's privacy policy governs what happens there.

You can change or withdraw location access at any time in iOS Settings → Privacy & Security → Location Services.

Class schedules

Juncture shows class timetables for some gyms and studios. Those timetables are published by us as plain files, one per city, and the app downloads the file for the city you are in or searching. The download tells the file's host (GitHub, which serves the files for us) your IP address and which city's file you asked for, as any web request does. It carries nothing else: no identifier, no location more precise than "which city", no health or calendar data. We do not see these requests. The timetables themselves are public information published by the studios; the files are signed so the app can tell they came from us.

Diagnostics

Juncture keeps a small record of its own failures so that bugs can be found and fixed: crash, hang and slow-launch reports from iOS's MetricKit system, plus failures the app handled internally — a saved file it could not read, a calendar write that did not go through.

These are stored in Juncture's own folder on your device, capped at the twelve most recent, and nothing is transmitted. There is no crash-reporting service, no third-party SDK, and no automatic upload. If you choose to send us a report to help with a bug, you do that deliberately — by copying it and emailing it to us — and we only ever see what you choose to send.

Separately, Apple may provide us with anonymised crash reports from users who have opted in to sharing diagnostics with developers at the iOS level. That is Apple's system, not ours; you control it in iOS Settings → Privacy & Security → Analytics & Improvements.

What Juncture stores on your device

That is the complete list. All of it is inside Juncture's own sandbox on your iPhone, where no other app can read it.

  • iOS Keychain: your Google Calendar refresh token, and only if you connected Google Calendar. Marked device-only, so it is excluded from iCloud backups.
  • App preferences: two yes/no flags — whether Juncture has already asked you for Health access, so it does not ask twice, and whether you have seen the first-run introduction, so it is not shown again.
  • A small file of today's decisions: which workout you booked, held or logged, whether you added the week ahead, whether you dismissed the sample-data notice, and today's date. These are facts about today, so they are discarded automatically when the date changes.
  • A training log: for each day on which Juncture made a recommendation using your own Health or calendar data, one record of what it recommended, the recovery figures it based that on (sleep hours, HRV, resting heart rate), and what happened — whether you opened a booking, said you booked, logged a workout, or a workout appeared in Apple Health later that day. Up to 365 days. This exists so Juncture can tell you how often its calls were taken, and it is the one thing in the app that accumulates. You can see it and delete it in Settings › Your history.
  • Saved venues: any gym or studio you bookmark, with its name, type, coordinates, street address, phone number and website as Apple Maps listed them, and when you saved it. Removable one at a time from the venue, or all at once in Settings.
  • Diagnostic reports: as described above, up to twelve.
  • A snapshot for the home-screen widget: today's recommendation — its name, the window and leave-by times, your readiness score, and whether you held or booked it — written to a shared container that only Juncture and its own widget can read, so the widget can show it without any access of its own. Overwritten each time the call changes.
  • A Live Activity while a window is held: the session name, venue and the window's times, shown by iOS in the Dynamic Island and on the lock screen as a countdown to leaving. It is created and updated on your phone only — no push notifications, no server — and ends when the window does.
  • One pending reminder, if you allow notifications: when you hold a window, Juncture asks iOS to show a single notification fifteen minutes before you need to leave for it, naming the session and the venue. It is scheduled and delivered on your phone by iOS; nothing about it is sent anywhere. Holding a different window replaces it, and the day's reset removes it. Notifications can be turned off in iOS Settings at any time.

Your health data and your calendar events are not in this list. Juncture does not keep its own copy of either. It reads them when it needs them and holds them in memory only while it is running.

Links to other apps and websites

From the booking flow, Juncture can open a studio's own booking page on the platform it uses (for example mindbodyonline.com or momence.com), the studio's website, ClassPass (classpass.com), or Apple Maps (maps.apple.com). Juncture does not take the booking itself — it decides what and when, then hands you over.

When you tap one of these, Juncture opens the destination and sends no information about you with it. Once you are there, you are dealing with that company, and their privacy policy governs what they collect. We have no affiliate tracking, no referral identifiers, and no way to tell whether you went on to book anything — which is why Juncture asks you afterwards instead of assuming.

What Juncture does not do

Stated plainly, because these absences are the point:

  • No accounts. You never create one, so there is nothing to sign in to.
  • No servers run by us. None. See above.
  • No analytics. We do not know how many people use Juncture, which screens they open, or what it recommends to them.
  • No advertising, no ad identifiers, no IDFA, no ad networks.
  • No tracking of you across other apps or websites, by any definition.
  • No third-party SDKs of any kind. Juncture is built entirely on Apple's own frameworks, plus direct calls to Google Calendar if you connect it.
  • No sale of data, and no data broker relationships. There is nothing to sell and no mechanism to sell it.

Keeping and deleting your information

Deleting Juncture deletes everything Juncture stored. Removing the app removes its sandbox, which takes with it the Keychain token, the preference flag, the daily decisions file and the diagnostic reports. Because there is no server, there is no second copy anywhere. Nothing survives.

Three honest qualifications:

  1. Your Health and Calendar data is not Juncture's to delete. It lives in Apple Health and in your calendars, which belong to you and to Apple. Deleting Juncture stops Juncture reading them; it does not remove them, and it should not.
  2. "(held by Juncture)" events stay in your calendar. Once written, those events are yours. Deleting the app does not remove them — delete them in your calendar app if you want them gone.
  3. A Google Calendar connection must be revoked at Google. Deleting Juncture removes the token from your phone, but the permission you granted is recorded in your Google account until you remove it there, as described above.

Short of deleting the app, you can withdraw any individual permission at any time in iOS Settings or the Health app, and Juncture will carry on working with less information.

Legal basis, if you are in the EEA or the UK

the developer has not determined whether the GDPR or the UK GDPR applies to it, and that determination depends on facts a lawyer should confirm — including where we are established and whether we are offering Juncture to people in the EU or UK. This section is written on the assumption that it may apply, and is flagged for legal review. We would rather be explicit about that than imply a certainty we do not have.

On that assumption:

  • Health data is special-category data under Article 9 of the GDPR. Heart-rate variability, resting heart rate, sleep and workout history all concern your physical health, and the law treats them as needing stronger protection than ordinary personal data. We handle them accordingly: read on your device, used on your device, never transmitted.
  • Our basis for processing health data would be your explicit consent (Article 9(2)(a)), given through the iOS Health permission prompt, which names what Juncture reads and why before you decide. Juncture does not read anything until you grant it, and you can withdraw at any time in the Health app, with no loss of the app's basic function.
  • For calendar and location, our basis would be your consent (Article 6(1)(a)), given through the corresponding iOS permission prompts, and equally withdrawable in Settings.
  • We are not certain we are a "controller" of most of this information, since we never receive it and have no ability to access it. Whether a developer whose app processes data entirely on the user's own device is a controller of that data is a real legal question, and it is one we have flagged for our lawyer rather than asserting an answer to. Where we are, we intend to meet the obligations that follow.
  • No international transfers by us. We do not transmit your information anywhere, so there is no transfer mechanism to disclose. If you connect Google Calendar, your calendar data moves between you and Google under Google's own terms and transfer arrangements, not ours.
  • No automated decision-making with legal effect. Juncture recommends a workout. It is a transparent set of rules, not a model, and it shows you its reasoning so you can disagree with it. It makes no decision about you that has any legal or comparable significance.

Health data under US state law

Several US states treat health information as a sensitive category requiring specific consent — including Washington's My Health My Data Act, Nevada's SB 370, and the sensitive-data provisions of the California Consumer Privacy Act and the comprehensive privacy laws of states such as Colorado, Connecticut and Virginia.

Whether any of these apply to the developer depends on facts we cannot resolve here: where we are established, whether we meet each statute's threshold for being covered, and — most importantly — whether reading data on a user's own device and never receiving it counts as "collecting" or "processing" it under each law. Some of these statutes define consumer health data very broadly. We have flagged this for legal review and are not asserting a conclusion.

What we can tell you is what we actually do, which is the same under every one of them: Juncture reads your health data on your phone, with your explicit permission, uses it only to recommend a workout, never transmits it, never sells it, never shares it, and never uses it for advertising. We do not sell or share personal information for cross-context behavioural advertising, because we do not transmit personal information at all.

Children

Juncture is not intended for children. We ask that you be at least 16 years old to use it.

We do not knowingly collect information from children, and because the app has no accounts and no servers, we do not receive information from anyone of any age. Juncture does not ask your age, and we have no way to verify it.

Because Juncture reads health data, a parent or guardian should think carefully before letting a child use it, and should be aware that training recommendations based on recovery signals are designed for adults.

If you believe a child has used Juncture in a way that concerns you, contact us at patfoley4291@gmail.com and we will help as far as we are able — though in practice there is no stored information for us to locate or delete.

Your rights

Depending on where you live, you may have the right to access your personal information, correct it, delete it, obtain a copy, object to its processing, or complain to a regulator.

We want to be straightforward about what we can and cannot do, because a policy that promises a data export we could never produce would be worse than useless.

We hold no information about you. No account, no identifier, no record that you have ever installed Juncture. So:

  • Access and portability. We have nothing to give you, because we have nothing. The information Juncture uses is already yours and already in your hands: your workouts, sleep and heart data are in the Apple Health app, which can export them; your events are in your calendar app; the handful of flags Juncture stores are on your phone.
  • Deletion. You delete everything Juncture stored by deleting the app, as described above. You do not need to ask us, and we could not do it for you even if you did.
  • Correction. Anything Juncture gets wrong, it gets wrong because of what is in Health or your calendar. Correct it there and Juncture's next recommendation follows.
  • Withdrawing consent. Revoke any permission in the Health app or iOS Settings, at any time, with immediate effect.
  • Objecting, or restricting processing. The processing happens on your device, under permissions you control. Revoking a permission stops it.
  • Non-discrimination. We could not treat you differently for exercising a right even if we wanted to, because we do not know who you are.

If you want to make a request anyway, raise a concern, or ask us a question about any of this, email patfoley4291@gmail.com and we will respond within 30 days. If you are in the EEA or the UK you also have the right to complain to your national data protection authority; if you are in the UK that is the Information Commissioner's Office.

If this ever changes — if a version of Juncture has accounts or a server — we will build a real rights process before shipping it, and say so here.

Questions for your lawyer

These are the specific things that could not be determined from the code and that need professional judgement. They are listed roughly in order of how much they could cost you to get wrong.

  1. Where are you established, and does that make the GDPR or UK GDPR apply? Everything in the "Legal basis" section is written conditionally because this is unresolved. If you are established in the EEA or UK, the GDPR applies to you regardless of where your users are. If you are established elsewhere — the US, say — it applies only if you are offering the app to people in the EU/UK, and the App Store makes you available in every territory you do not deliberately exclude. Ask specifically whether you should restrict App Store availability at launch to narrow this, and whether Article 27 obliges you to appoint an EU or UK representative. That last one has a real cost and a real penalty, so get a direct answer rather than an assumption.

  2. Are you a "controller" of data your app never receives? This is the question the whole policy turns on. Juncture processes special-category health data on the user's device and transmits none of it. Regulators have not settled whether a developer in that position is a controller, a processor, or outside the framework for that data. The answer determines whether you owe records of processing under Article 30, a DPIA under Article 35 (plausibly triggered by large-scale processing of health data, if it is your processing at all), and whether the rights section above is adequate or merely polite. Do not let this be answered by analogy to a cloud app; the on-device fact pattern is the whole point.

  3. Does Washington's My Health My Data Act reach you? It defines consumer health data broadly, requires separate consent for collection and a distinct consent for sharing, requires its own standalone Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy — a separate document from this one, not a section of it — and it carries a private right of action, which is why it is the US statute most likely to actually hurt. The threshold question is whether reading data on the user's device constitutes "collecting" it under the Act. Ask whether you need the separate MHMDA policy at all, and if the answer is anything other than a clear no, write it. Nevada's SB 370 is similar and worth asking about in the same breath.

  4. What is your age floor, and how do you justify it? COPPA attaches to children under 13 in the US, but so do the App Store's age-rating rules, the UK Age Appropriate Design Code, and the GDPR's Article 8 digital-consent age — which varies by member state between 13 and 16. You have no accounts and collect nothing, which helps a great deal, but you are handling health data and giving training advice, which cuts the other way. Decide on 13, 16 or 18, make the App Store age rating consistent with it, and fill in the placeholder. Ask whether an age gate is needed or whether a stated minimum is sufficient given that you collect nothing.

  5. Is "we are not a joint controller with Google" right? When a user connects Google Calendar, your app causes their calendar data to move to and from Google and writes events into their account. You never see it. But you chose the scopes, you initiate the calls, and you determine the purpose. Confirm whether that makes you a controller or joint controller of that processing, and whether the Google section above needs to say more than it does. Related: you will also need Google's OAuth verification before you can offer this to more than 100 users, and their review will ask for a hosted privacy policy that specifically discloses your use of Google user data and your compliance with their Limited Use requirements. Ask your lawyer to check this policy against those requirements before you submit for verification — the language Google wants is specific and this draft does not contain it.

  6. Are the HealthKit commitments phrased strongly enough for Apple? Apple's App Review Guidelines (currently §5.1.3) prohibit using HealthKit data for advertising, marketing or similar services, and prohibit sharing it with third parties without consent. This draft states all of that. Have someone confirm the wording satisfies Apple as written, because a rejection here costs a review cycle, and the usual cause is a policy that is silent rather than one that is wrong.

  7. Is a liability or "not medical advice" disclaimer needed, and does it belong here? Juncture tells people how hard to train based on their heart-rate variability and sleep. That is not a privacy question, which is why it is not in the policy above, but it is the other document you are missing. Ask whether you need terms of use with a medical disclaimer and limitation of liability, and whether any of it needs to be surfaced in the app rather than only on the web.

  8. Does your policy need to be reachable from inside the app, and in what form? Apple requires the URL in App Store Connect. For HealthKit apps, reviewers commonly expect it to be reachable in-app too. The app currently has no link to it anywhere — see the implementation notes. Confirm what is required versus merely advisable before you decide how much UI to build.
